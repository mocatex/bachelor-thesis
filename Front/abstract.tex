\begin{abstract}
    Modern astronomical surveys such as the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) produce
    millions of optical spectra, and classical deep learning already classifies them
    with high accuracy. This thesis asks whether quantum machine learning offers any
    benefit on this task under a fair comparison, with matched parameters and identical
    pipelines, against classical models built with equal care. We first train a
    14-million-parameter convolutional baseline that reaches 84.5\% accuracy on a
    62-class SDSS problem and matches the GaSNet-II deep-learning benchmark at 92.5\% on a
    13-class subset. We then evaluate quantum methods in four experiments, namely a
    variational quantum circuit on the hardest binary pair, parameter-matched quantum
    and classical heads on a frozen feature extractor, quantum support vector machines
    in a few-shot regime and a quanvolutional filter under increasing noise. None of
    them shows a quantum advantage. The quantum and classical models reach statistically
    equivalent accuracy, and the advantages that first appeared were traced to an
    under-built classical baseline or a dead-ReLU optimisation artefact rather than to
    the quantum component. Since all circuits run on a noiseless simulator, the most
    favourable setting for quantum hardware, the absence of an advantage is a robust
    result. The thesis contributes a disciplined fair-comparison methodology and shows
    that apparent quantum advantages must be checked against carefully built classical
    controls.
\end{abstract}
