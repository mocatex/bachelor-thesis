\begin{abstract}
    Modern astronomical surveys such as the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) produce
    millions of optical spectra. Classical deep learning already solves the coarse
    star-galaxy-quasar classification with high accuracy. This thesis asks
    whether quantum machine learning adds anything once the task is pushed to the hard,
    fine-grained subclasses where classical models begin to struggle. It insists on a
    fair comparison where every quantum model is matched in trainable parameters and run
    through an identical pipeline against a classical counterpart built with equal care.
    We first train a 14-million-parameter convolutional baseline that reaches 84.5\%
    accuracy on a 62-class SDSS problem and reproduces the GaSNet-II benchmark at 92.5\%
    on the same 13-class subset used in that study. This baseline also identifies the
    hardest class pairs and serves as a frozen feature extractor. We then run five
    experiments, namely a variational quantum circuit
    on the hardest binary pair, parameter-matched quantum and classical heads on the
    frozen extractor, a quantum support vector machine in a few-shot regime, a
    quanvolutional filter under increasing noise and a fully coherent quantum
    convolutional network (QCNN) as a negative control. Across all five the result is
    parity at best and no scalable quantum advantage. Two apparent advantages disappeared
    on closer inspection, one caused by an under-built classical baseline and one by a
    dead-ReLU artefact that a single change of activation removed. The only case where a
    quantum model did better was the QCNN, which held its accuracy when training data was
    scarce while the equally small classical model failed. This came from its small and
    rigid design helping it learn from few examples, not from any ability to represent
    richer functions or scale to larger problems. Because all
    circuits run on a noiseless statevector simulator, the most favourable setting for
    the variational methods studied here, the absence of an advantage is a conservative
    result. The thesis contributes a disciplined fair-comparison methodology and shows
    that apparent quantum advantages must be treated as hypotheses to be ruled out
    against carefully built classical controls.
\end{abstract}
