\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
\PARstart{T}{he} Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) has measured more than two
million optical spectra of stars, galaxies and quasars~\cite{york2000sdss}, each
containing thousands of flux measurements across a range of wavelengths, effectively a
one-dimensional signal encoding the composition, temperature, redshift and class
of its source. Our starting point was to establish strong classical baselines for
classifying these spectra. The familiar three-way task of separating stars,
galaxies and quasars is already essentially solved by deep learning. Modern models
such as GaSNet-II reach roughly 99\% accuracy on this coarse
problem~\cite{zhong2024gasnet}. Even earlier convolutional networks classify stellar
spectra directly from the flux~\cite{sharma2020cnn}. The same line of work has pushed
the problem harder by scaling up to 13 classes~\cite{zhong2024gasnet}.
We therefore took the problem one step further, to fine-grained
subclasses, where accuracy drops at the boundaries between physically similar types
such as late-type M dwarfs and L brown dwarfs. These two classes sit right next to each
other on a continuous cooling sequence. The change from M to L is gradual. The TiO and
VO absorption bands that dominate late-M spectra slowly fade and are replaced by metal
hydrides and neutral alkali lines~\cite{kirkpatrick1999ldwarfs}. Because this transition
is so smooth, the two classes end up looking almost the same.
Figure~\ref{fig:ml_similarity} shows this on our own data, where the mean SDSS spectra
of the L brown dwarfs and the M8 dwarfs overlap across almost the whole optical range.
In machine-learning terms the two classes occupy almost the same region of feature
space, which is why a classifier has a hard time separating them. These hard cases raise
the question that drives this thesis, whether quantum machine learning can add anything
useful where strong classical models begin to struggle.

A Variational Quantum Circuit (VQC) is a circuit whose gate rotation angles are
trained by gradient descent~\cite{perezsalinas2020reuploading}. Its $n$ qubits act on a
Hilbert space of dimension $2^n$. Entangling gates let the circuit represent
correlations across those qubits. We approach it as an
empirical and deliberately cautious investigation rather than an assumption of
quantum superiority. In our experiments a purely quantum model never trained well
on raw spectra, a difficulty consistent with known trainability obstacles in
variational circuits~\cite{mcclean2018barren}. Every quantum model we study is
therefore hybrid, combining a classical feature stage with a small quantum component.
All circuits run on
a noiseless statevector simulator~\cite{bergholm2018pennylane} with eight qubits or
fewer. We make no claim about real quantum hardware. The guiding question is
whether such hybrids offer any measurable benefit over comparably built classical
models on the hardest SDSS tasks and because these comparisons are sensitive to
small implementation choices we treat every result carefully.

The thesis makes three contributions. First, we train a large classical
CNN-Transformer baseline of about 14 million parameters, which we nickname the
\emph{Beast}. It reaches around 84\% accuracy on a curated 62-class problem and
reproduces the GaSNet-II benchmark at about 92\% on the same 13-class subset used in
that study~\cite{zhong2024gasnet}, confirming that our classical side is competitive
with the published state of the art. Its confusion structure identifies the hardest
class pairs. The same network is reused as a frozen feature extractor in the later
experiments.

Second, we run a series of fair comparisons in which every quantum model is matched in
parameters and surrounding pipeline to a classical counterpart built with equal care.
On the hardest binary confusion pair a VQC and a classical convolutional network of
matched size and depth reach the same accuracy. On a four-class task a 556-parameter hybrid quantum
head matches both a parameter-matched classical head and a nine-times-larger classical
head at about 96\% accuracy. An apparent quantum edge over the small classical
baseline turned out to be a dead-ReLU artefact that a change of activation fully
removed. We extend the same comparison to two further quantum paradigms, a quantum
support vector machine in a few-shot regime and a quanvolutional
filter~\cite{henderson2020quanvolutional} under increasing input noise. Neither shows a
quantum advantage. The quantum kernels are matched or overtaken by a classical kernel
as the training set grows. The quanvolutional filter is statistically
indistinguishable from a parameter-matched classical filter, with the only significant
difference favouring the classical model. Finally, a fully coherent quantum
convolutional network (QCNN) serves as a negative control. With the spectrum embedded
classically it shows no advantage over an unconstrained classical network. The one place
it beats a parameter-matched classical model is the few-shot regime, where its small and
rigid design helps it learn from very little data rather than giving it any ability to
scale.

Third, and most important, our contribution is the fair-comparison methodology itself.
The recurring lesson is that an apparent quantum advantage must be treated as a
hypothesis to be ruled out rather than a result, since in two separate experiments it
dissolved once the classical baseline was given the same care. Parameter count alone is
also the wrong lens, since the saving we might have credited to the quantum head in fact
belongs to the frozen feature extractor.

This work is an empirical study rather than a development of new quantum
algorithms. It evaluates only on SDSS spectra and noiseless simulation and makes no
claim of a general quantum advantage or general parameter efficiency. The full
implementation is released as a reproducibility
package.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/ShpetimV/QML-AnalyzingGalaxies}} The
remainder of the thesis reviews the necessary background, describes the data and
methodology, presents the baseline and experimental results and closes with a
discussion and outlook.
