\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
\PARstart{T}{he} Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) is a multi-decade astronomical
survey that has measured more than two million optical spectra of stars, galaxies
and quasars~\cite{york2000sdss}. Each spectrum is a one-dimensional
intensity-versus-wavelength signal that encodes the chemical composition,
temperature, redshift and astrophysical class of its source. Such data feeds
research in stellar population studies, galaxy evolution, quasar demographics and
large-scale structure cosmology. Turning raw spectra into usable scientific labels
at this scale is difficult, since manual labelling cannot keep pace with the rate
at which spectra are collected, hence the need for automated classifications.

Modern classical deep learning already handles the coarse problem comfortably.
Deciding whether a spectrum belongs to a star, a galaxy or a quasar is close to
solved by current neural spectral classifiers~\cite{zhong2024gasnet}, and in our
own preliminary experiments even modest classical networks reach roughly 99\%
accuracy on this three-way classification. The interesting difficulty lies one
level deeper. When the task is refined from \emph{what kind of object} to
\emph{which exact subtype}, performance degrades sharply at the boundaries between
physically similar sub-classes. Late-type M dwarfs against L brown dwarfs, or
star-forming against starburst galaxies, are separated by subtle and overlapping
spectral features. These hard confusion pairs are exactly the regime in which
extra model capacity or a different inductive bias might still pay off, and they
motivate the central question of this thesis.

Quantum machine learning proposes that parameterised quantum circuits can
represent functions whose classical approximation would require substantially more
parameters. A Variational Quantum Circuit (VQC) is a quantum circuit whose gates
carry trainable rotation angles~\cite{perezsalinas2020reuploading}. These angles
are optimised by classical gradient descent, much like the weights of a neural
network, while the circuit itself exploits the exponentially large Hilbert space
($2^n$ dimensions for $n$ qubits) and the entangling structure of multi-qubit
gates. On near-term hardware the realistic ambition is not asymptotic quantum
advantage but the more modest claim that a small VQC, combined with classical pre-
and post-processing, can match a larger classical model on a specific task.
Testing even this claim fairly is delicate, because an apparent quantum advantage
can easily turn out to be an artefact of an under-trained classical baseline rather
than a property of the quantum circuit. In this work all quantum models run on a
noiseless classical statevector simulator~\cite{bergholm2018pennylane} with eight
qubits or fewer, matching what is realistic on present-day hardware. We make no
claim about asymptotic advantage and run no experiments on physical quantum
devices.

This thesis addresses one deliberately controlled question. Under strict
parameter-matching and identical training conditions, does a VQC offer measurable
benefits over a classical model of equal capacity on hard SDSS classification
tasks? Rather than comparing against an arbitrary classical network, every quantum
model is benchmarked against a classical mirror with the same trainable-parameter
budget and an otherwise identical surrounding pipeline, meaning the same data,
preprocessing, feature extractor, classifier head, optimiser and random seeds.
This discipline lets any observed difference be attributed to the contested
component itself rather than to incidental differences in scale or tuning.
Concretely we ask whether a small VQC outperforms a parameter-matched classical
model on a hard binary confusion pair and, if a gap appears in a na{\"i}ve setup,
what causes it; whether a VQC attached as a small hybrid head on a strong frozen
feature extractor can match a substantially larger classical head; and how the
geometry of the VQC and the choice of input encoding affect both accuracy and
per-class recall balance.

Our investigation rests on a common SDSS pipeline that consolidates noisy,
long-tailed raw subclass labels into a curated 62-class problem of roughly 200,000
spectra, on a strict fair-comparison discipline, and on what we treat as an equally
important honesty discipline. When an apparent quantum advantage first surfaced on
our hardest binary task, it was not reported as a result but diagnosed, traced to a
pathological optimisation issue in the classical baseline, corrected and
re-measured. Once classical baselines are properly parameter-matched and these
artefacts are removed, the VQC and the classical models turn out to be
statistically tied on top-line metrics, and the genuinely quantum-specific effects
we report are deliberately the modest ones that survive this scrutiny.

The thesis makes three contributions. First, and most substantially, we build a
large classical CNN-Transformer baseline of about 14 million parameters, which we
call the Beast, train it on the full 62-class problem where it reaches around 84\%
top-1 accuracy, and use it as the basis for a systematic analysis of the weak
classes where fine-grained spectral classification actually breaks down. This
analysis pinpoints the hard confusion pairs and exposes the minority-class recall
imbalance that a single accuracy figure hides, and the frozen Beast then doubles as
a 128-dimensional feature extractor for the quantum experiments. Second, we present
a controlled comparison of small VQCs against parameter-matched classical models on
these hard classes. A 556-parameter hybrid VQC head matches a roughly
ten-times-larger classical head at equal accuracy while a same-size purely
classical control fails to reproduce the result, isolating the contribution to the
quantum circuit, and within the VQC family wide-shallow circuits cut the
minority-class recall gap from roughly 23 to 8 percentage points against the
conventional narrow-and-deep heuristic associated with barren
plateaus~\cite{mcclean2018barren}. Along the way we document how dead-ReLU gradient
starvation in a narrow classical bottleneck can manufacture an illusory quantum
advantage, and we question whether counting trainable parameters is even a fair
axis of comparison, since one VQC layer entangles all qubits over an exponentially
large state space and 500 quantum parameters need not be the moral equivalent of
500 classical ones. Third, we turn from parameter efficiency to robustness and ask
how the two paradigms cope when the data itself degrades. We implement a
quanvolutional neural network, in which a small quantum circuit is slid across the
spectrum as a learnable convolutional filter, encoding each local patch through
data re-uploading and entangling gates and reading it out in several measurement
bases to form its output feature channels~\cite{henderson2020quanvolutional}. This
quantum filter is pitted against a classical convolutional filter under
progressively increasing input noise in two complementary settings, one in which
the quantum and classical filters are held at a comparable parameter budget and one
in which the quantum filter faces a deliberately strong classical convolutional
network with no attempt to match parameter counts at all. The second setting is the
more demanding stress test, since it sets the parameter-counting lens aside
entirely and asks only whether the quantum model can keep up with a genuinely
capable classical competitor as noise grows. Across the noise sweep the quantum
filter degrades slightly more gracefully than both classical baselines, a small but
consistent advantage that we report with confidence intervals rather than as single
point estimates, so that the size of the effect can be weighed against its
uncertainty.

This thesis focuses on controlled empirical comparison rather than on new quantum
algorithms, and the full reproducibility package of data pipeline, architectures,
training scripts and logged runs is made publicly
available.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/ShpetimV/QML-AnalyzingGalaxies}} We do
not demonstrate asymptotic quantum advantage, we do not evaluate on physical
quantum hardware or model device noise, and our findings are specific to SDSS
spectra, limitations that are stated plainly and revisited in the discussion.

The remainder of this thesis is organised as follows. The next section reviews the
background on SDSS spectra, classical deep learning and variational quantum
circuits. We then describe the data pipeline, the model architectures and the
fair-comparison and training protocol, before presenting the results of the
classical baseline and the experiments that follow. The findings, their limitations
and their implications are then discussed, before the thesis concludes with an
outlook on future work.
