This section reports the results of the conducted experiments. The model architectures, the

\subsection{Classical CNN Baseline Performance (62 Classes)}
The Baseline CNN referred to throughout this section is the CNN-Transformer of
Section~\ref{sec:baseline_cnn}, the network we nickname the Beast, whose architecture is
shown in Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_cnn}. It was trained for 300 epochs on the 62-class SDSS DR19 dataset. The network achieved a final test accuracy of $84.50\%$, with a final test loss of 0.2479. The training history indicates continuous convergence, with the training accuracy peaking at $91.69\%$ and validation accuracy stabilizing around $84.40\%$ by the final epoch (Figure~\ref{fig:cnn_baseline_history}).

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\columnwidth]{Figures/classical_training_history.png}
    \caption{Training and validation accuracy and loss for the 62-class Baseline CNN over 300 epochs.}
    \label{fig:cnn_baseline_history}
\end{figure}

Due to its high dimensionality, the complete $62 \times 62$ normalized confusion matrix is provided in Appendix A. A targeted class-wise analysis reveals significant performance disparities. The lowest recall rates were observed in fine-grained subclasses, specifically \texttt{STAR\_A5} ($35.2\%$), \texttt{QSO\_STARFORMING} ($38.8\%$), \texttt{QSO\_STARBURST} ($40.8\%$), and \texttt{STAR\_SUBDWARF\_F} ($45.7\%$). Furthermore, precision metrics highlight specific categories acting as attractors for false positives, most notably \texttt{GALAXY\_BROADLINE} ($35.2\%$ precision, absorbing 306 false positives) and \texttt{STAR\_SUBDWARF\_F} ($42.3\%$ precision, absorbing 123 false positives).

The classification matrix reveals strictly symmetric topological overlaps (``mirrors'') between specific pairs in the feature space. The most severe symmetrical confusions occurred between \texttt{STAR\_A5} and \texttt{STAR\_F0} ($33.7\%$ total overlap), \texttt{QSO\_STARBURST} and \texttt{QSO\_STARBURST\_BROADLINE} ($28.7\%$ total overlap), and \texttt{QSO\_STARBURST\_BROADLINE} and \texttt{QSO\_STARFORMING} ($25.2\%$ total overlap).

\subsection{Validation against Literature Benchmark: GaSNet-II Replica (13 Classes)}
\label{sec:gasnet_replica}
To validate the feature extraction capability of our 14-million parameter architecture
against an established literature baseline, we reproduced the 13-class classification
\emph{task} proposed by Zhong et al. (2024) in the GaSNet-II study
\cite{Zhong2024GaSNetII}. This benchmark leverages a distinct subset of SDSS spectra
restricted to 13 high-population, coarse-grained subclasses. It is important to be precise
about what is being reproduced here: we did not reimplement the GaSNet-II network. The
model trained in this section is our own Baseline CNN, the same architecture we nickname
the Beast in Section~\ref{sec:baseline_cnn} and depict in Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_cnn},
instantiated with a 13-way output head and trained from scratch on their data. The only
other change is the input width, since the GaSNet-II spectra are supplied on a 3600-bin
grid rather than the 4096 bins used for our own subset. We adopt their dataset and class
definitions and ask whether our architecture reaches a comparable accuracy on them, which
makes this a reproduction of the benchmark setting rather than a replication of their
model.

Operating on the GaSNet-II dataset, our network was trained for 75 epochs. The model achieved a final test accuracy of $92.51\%$ and a test loss of 0.2311, effectively matching the benchmark performance criteria. The training metrics demonstrated rapid early convergence, reaching a validation accuracy of $90.41\%$ by epoch 20 (Figure~\ref{fig:gasnet_history}).

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\columnwidth]{Figures/gasnet_training_history.png}
    \caption{Training and validation accuracy and loss for the 13-class GaSNet-II replication over 75 epochs.}
    \label{fig:gasnet_history}
\end{figure}

The normalized confusion matrix for the 13-class validation task is presented in Figure~\ref{fig:gasnet_cm}. Diagonal values demonstrate robust class separation, with true positive rates strictly exceeding $85\%$ across all categories. Peak classification performances were recorded for \texttt{STAR\_G2} ($97.5\%$), \texttt{STAR\_K5} ($97.2\%$), and \texttt{STAR\_A0} ($96.8\%$). The highest inter-class misclassification rate occurred between adjacent stellar temperatures, with $9.5\%$ of \texttt{STAR\_F9} spectra erroneously classified as \texttt{STAR\_G2}.

\subsubsection{Top-line Parity}

The headline is parity. The best quantum configuration, 4 qubits with 6 layers, and
the best classical configuration, four features, sit within one standard deviation
of each other on both accuracy and macro-F1, at 0.869 against 0.868 accuracy and
0.840 against 0.843 macro-F1. No configuration meaningfully outperforms another on
top-line metrics, and the classical four-feature mirror in fact takes the highest
ROC-AUC at 0.933. There is therefore no quantum accuracy advantage on this task,
which also retires the large gap seen in an early uncontrolled run before the
classical baseline was built with a robust activation.

\subsubsection{Circuit Geometry and Class Balance}

The more interesting pattern lies within the quantum family. As the circuit moves
from narrow and deep, 2 qubits with 12 layers, to wide and shallow, 8 qubits with 3
layers, recall on the minority class \texttt{STAR\_BROWN\_DWARF\_L} climbs steadily
from 0.70 to 0.80 while recall on \texttt{STAR\_M8} falls from 0.93 to 0.89. The
recall gap between the two classes shrinks from 23.2 to 8.3 percentage points, and
the wide-shallow circuit also has the smallest seed-to-seed variation on the
minority recall, so it is reliably the most balanced classifier at only a small cost
to overall accuracy. This runs against the usual expectation, since wider circuits
are normally associated with barren-plateau trainability problems, yet here the
wide-shallow shape both trains well and balances the classes best. We regard this
balance behaviour as the most defensible quantum-specific finding of the experiment.
The representative confusion matrices in Figure~\ref{fig:exp1_cm} show the same
picture, with the residual leakage concentrated on the minority class.

\subsubsection{Training Dynamics}

Figure~\ref{fig:exp1_training} shows representative single-run training histories for
the quantum and the classical model. In both, the training loss and training
accuracy descend smoothly and almost monotonically, while the validation curves are
markedly spiky, with validation accuracy swinging between roughly 0.30 and 0.85 from
one epoch to the next. It is tempting to read these spikes as a barren plateau, the
vanishing-gradient pathology of wide random circuits, but the evidence points
elsewhere. A barren plateau would flatten the training curve, since the model would
receive almost no gradient and would not learn, whereas here the training loss falls
smoothly and the model reaches about 0.87 test accuracy, so informative gradients are
clearly present. The instability is confined to the validation metric, and it
appears just as strongly in the purely classical mirror, so it cannot be a quantum
trainability effect. The more likely cause is the combination of a very narrow
feature bottleneck, two heavily overlapping classes and batch-normalisation
statistics, where small per-epoch weight changes move the decision boundary enough
to flip many borderline spectra at once. The smooth training curves and the solid
final test metrics confirm that the models train successfully, and that the spikes
are noise in the model-selection metric rather than a failure to learn.

\subsubsection{Summary of Findings}

Experiment 1 finds no quantum accuracy advantage on the hardest binary pair. Across
the parameter-matched sweep the best quantum and the best classical configurations
are statistically tied on accuracy and macro-F1, and the classical mirror even takes
the highest ROC-AUC, so the early uncontrolled gap does not survive a properly built
baseline. The one quantum-specific pattern worth reporting is architectural rather
than about accuracy, since within the quantum family the wide-shallow circuit is
reliably the most balanced classifier and halves the minority-class recall gap
relative to the narrow-deep circuit, against the barren-plateau expectation for wider
circuits. The spiky validation curves are not evidence of a barren plateau, because
training proceeds smoothly and the instability is just as strong in the classical
mirror. Finally, the quantum circuit carries only 72 of about 24500 trainable
parameters, with the shared CNN extractor doing the heavy lifting, which is why the
comparison had to be matched at the contested middle stage and why a large quantum
advantage was never plausible in this regime.

\subsection{Experiment 2: Quantum and Classical Heads on Frozen Features}
\label{sec:exp2}

Coming from the prior experiments, where we found no advantage for quantum models
over classical solutions, we push the problem harder and ask whether a quantum head
can be more parameter-efficient than a classical one on a hard four-class spectral
task. The four classes are \texttt{STAR\_BROWN\_DWARF\_L}, \texttt{STAR\_M8},
\texttt{GALAXY\_STARBURST} and \texttt{GALAXY\_STARFORMING}, balanced to 1000 samples
per class and split $70/15/15$ into train, validation and test. As detailed in
Section~\ref{sec:exp2_methods}, every head sits on the same frozen feature extractor
and receives the same 128-dimensional feature vector, so any difference comes from
the head alone. The four heads are the dense classical baseline (Model~A, 5058
parameters), the hybrid quantum head (Model~B, 556 parameters) and two
parameter-matched classical controls with a ReLU or a $\tanh$ middle stage
(Models~C and~D, 556 parameters each). Models~C and~D share the exact shape and
parameter count of Model~B, so the comparison isolates the quantum circuit rather
than the surrounding structure.

\subsubsection{Overall Classification Performance}

Table~\ref{tab:exp2_headline} summarises the headline results and
Figure~\ref{fig:exp2_cm} shows the corresponding test confusion matrices. The dense
classical head (A), the quantum head (B) and the Tanh control (D) all reach roughly
96\% overall accuracy with very similar per-class behaviour, while the ReLU control
(C) collapses to about 79\%. The three successful models classify the hardest
class, \texttt{STAR\_BROWN\_DWARF\_L}, with recall between 0.93 and 0.95 and confuse
it almost only with \texttt{STAR\_M8}, the spectrally adjacent class. Notably the
556-parameter quantum head (B) and the 556-parameter Tanh control (D) match the
$9\times$ larger 5058-parameter dense head (A) at the same accuracy, which shows
that a tiny head is sufficient once the features are supplied by a strong frozen
extractor.

\subsubsection{The Parameter-Matched Control and the Dead-ReLU Artefact}

The decisive comparison is between the three 556-parameter models. The quantum head
(B) reaches 0.93 recall on \texttt{STAR\_BROWN\_DWARF\_L}, but the ReLU control (C)
reaches only 0.18 and routes about 75\% of brown dwarfs into
\texttt{GALAXY\_STARFORMING}, as seen in Figure~\ref{fig:exp2_cm_relu}, which caps
its overall accuracy near 79\%. Taken on its own this looks like a clear quantum
advantage on the hardest class. The Tanh control (D), which is identical to C except
that the inner ReLU is replaced by Tanh, fully recovers to 0.94 recall and about
96\% overall accuracy. This shows that the failure of C was not a capacity limit but
an optimisation artefact.

The mechanism is a dead-ReLU effect. With only four neurons in the middle layer, a
ReLU unit that is pushed into its negative region outputs zero and receives zero
gradient, so it never recovers, and losing even one or two of four units destroys
the decision boundary for the minority class. The training curves in
Figure~\ref{fig:exp2_training} make this visible. The ReLU control sits on a flat
plateau near 50\% accuracy from roughly epoch 5 to epoch 13 before only partially
escaping, whereas the Tanh control, whose activation always has a nonzero gradient,
trains cleanly and converges by about epoch 20. Once this confound is removed the
quantum head (B) and the classical Tanh head (D) are equal on every measured axis.

\subsubsection{Encoding Ablation}

A separate set of variants probes whether the limitation lies in the number of
qubits or in how the features are loaded into them. Table~\ref{tab:exp2_encoding}
compares the learned linear encoding of Model~B against unsupervised PCA encodings.
A frozen PCA projection into the circuit caps accuracy near 76\% regardless of its
width, while the learned \texttt{Linear(128,4)} encoding into the same four qubits
reaches 96\%. PCA selects directions of maximum variance, which need not coincide
with directions of class separability, so it wastes the four encoding slots. A
learned linear layer instead selects discriminative directions end-to-end through
the loss. The bottleneck is therefore which four numbers enter the circuit, not the
four-qubit count, which identifies data encoding rather than circuit width as the
limiting factor for the quantum model.

\subsubsection{Training Dynamics and Computational Cost}

Beyond final accuracy we also examined convergence, stability and cost, since
parameter count alone is not a sufficient basis for comparison. The classical heads
are no slower to converge than the quantum head and are in fact faster, with the
dense head settling by about epoch 7 and the Tanh control by about epoch 20, against
about epoch 25 for the quantum head. All models show spiky training loss late in
training, which is a stochastic-gradient effect on small batches, but their
validation loss is smooth and monotonic, so the noise is in the optimiser rather
than in generalisation. The generalisation gap is essentially zero for the
556-parameter heads, as expected given the frozen backbone. The quantum head is far
more expensive to train, since each forward and backward pass is a statevector
simulation of the circuit that costs seconds per epoch on CPU, against milliseconds
for the classical heads, and on real quantum hardware it would additionally face
shot noise and decoherence.

\subsubsection{Summary of Findings}

At matched parameter count the quantum head and the classical head reach the same
accuracy and the same per-class behaviour, so the result is quantum-classical parity
rather than a quantum advantage. The roughly $9\times$ saving of the 556-parameter
heads over the 5058-parameter dense head is real but is a property of the frozen
feature extractor, since the classical Tanh control obtains the same saving. The
single largest accuracy swing in the experiment, from 79\% to 96\% at identical
parameter count, came from the choice of activation function rather than from the
quantum or classical nature of the head, which supports the view that parameter
count alone is the wrong lens for these comparisons. We find no quantum advantage in
accuracy, parameter efficiency, convergence speed or training stability, and the
quantum model is considerably more costly to train. The methodological lesson is
that a variational quantum result should not be reported without a parameter-matched
classical control that uses a robust activation, because a width-limited ReLU
baseline can fail for reasons unrelated to capacity and create a false advantage.

\subsection{Experiment 3: Few-Shot Learning with Quantum Support Vector Machines (QSVM)}
\label{sec:exp3_results}

\subsubsection{Feature Selection and Dimensionality Reduction}
Prior to evaluating the support vector machines, a dimensionality reduction sweep was
conducted to determine how many pooled features to encode. As described in
Section~\ref{sec:exp3_encoding}, these features are not learned: the raw 4096-bin flux is
reduced by parameter-free adaptive average pooling to $N_{\text{features}}$ bins, one per
qubit, so this sweep also fixes the size of the quantum register. Five widths were run,
$f \in \{4, 6, 8, 12, 16\}$, each over the same four classes, the same seven training-set
sizes and the same eight seeds, so the runs are directly comparable
(Figure~\ref{fig:qsvm_feature_sweep}).

All three models improve steeply up to $f=12$ and then stop improving. Averaged over all
training-set sizes, going from $f=8$ to $f=12$ gains $+0.014$ accuracy for the classical
RBF kernel and $+0.031$ for the fidelity kernel, whereas going from $f=12$ to $f=16$ gains
only $+0.001$ and $+0.003$ respectively, neither significant under a paired Wilcoxon
signed-rank test ($p=0.47$ and $p=0.13$, 56 paired measurements). The projected kernel
peaks at $f=12$ and is slightly \emph{worse} at $f=16$ ($-0.006$, $p=0.11$), consistent
with the kernel concentration expected as the register grows. Since the cost of simulating
the register grows exponentially in the qubit count while accuracy does not, $f=12$ is the
natural operating point. All subsequent QSVM and classical SVM baselines were therefore
standardized to 12 input features mapped to a 12-qubit quantum register.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\columnwidth]{Figures/qsvm_feature_sweep.png}
    \caption{Classification accuracy as a function of the number of pooled features
        $N_{\text{features}}$, which equals the number of qubits. Left: averaged over all
        seven training-set sizes. Right: at the largest budget of $n=200$ samples per

    \label{fig:qsvm_feature_sweep}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Geometric Difference Pre-Screening (Phase 1)}
To assess the potential for quantum advantage before extensive model training, we computed
the geometric difference metric $g(K_C \| K_Q)$ between the classical RBF kernel and each
quantum kernel at the reported encoding bandwidth ($c = 0.5$, selected in Phase 2). For the
fidelity kernel, $g(K_C \| K_Q) = 3.95$ with an off-diagonal standard deviation of
$0.2149$, constant across all regularization scales $\lambda$ ($10^{-6}$ to $10^{-10}$).
For the projected kernel, $g(K_C \| K_Q) = 17.68$ with an off-diagonal standard deviation
of $0.2651$. Values approaching $1$ indicate that the quantum kernel is effectively
equivalent to the classical kernel up to a trivial reparameterization, rendering a
practical quantum advantage impossible \cite{huang2021power}.

Neither kernel sits in that degenerate regime at this bandwidth, so the pre-screen does
\emph{not} rule out an advantage in advance; on this diagnostic alone both quantum kernels
remain candidates. The screen is nonetheless informative in the opposite direction. Its
value depends strongly on the encoding bandwidth, falling to $g = 1.89$ for the fidelity
kernel at $c = 0.25$, so a small geometric difference here reflects a choice of encoding
rather than an intrinsic property of the data. More importantly, the empirical results of
Phase 2 show that this remaining geometric distinctness buys nothing: the projected kernel
is the more distinct of the two ($g = 17.68$) and the weaker of the two in accuracy. A
large geometric difference is therefore necessary but clearly not sufficient for a
practical separation, which is the main lesson we draw from the pre-screening.

\subsubsection{Scaling Sweep and Hyperparameter Tuning (Phase 2)}
A multi-seed scaling sweep was conducted across varying training sample sizes ($n \in \{2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200\}$ samples per class) to evaluate few-shot learning capabilities. We compared a classical SVM with a Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel against two QSVM variants: a fidelity kernel and a projected kernel. With $N_{\text{features}}$ fixed at 12, the remaining free quantity is the encoding bandwidth $c$ of the squashing function of Section~\ref{sec:exp3_encoding}, which controls how much of the available angular range the data occupies. The sweep was repeated over $c \in \{0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0, 1.5\}$. Note that $c$ is the encoding bandwidth and must not be confused with the SVM regularisation parameter $C$ of Section~\ref{sec:exp3_protocol}, which is a separate quantity.

Because the headline result of this experiment is a negative one, we report the
comparison at the bandwidth most favourable to the quantum kernels rather than at an
arbitrary or a convenient one, so that the conclusion is stated at the challenger's best
setting. Table~\ref{tab:qsvm_bandwidth} summarises the sweep. The fidelity kernel, the
stronger of the two quantum models throughout, peaks in the few-shot regime at $c = 0.5$,
and that bandwidth is also where the projected kernel attains its best large-sample
accuracy; both degrade in either direction, towards kernel concentration as $c$ grows and
towards an over-compressed encoding as $c$ shrinks. All results below are therefore
reported at $c = 0.5$. We stress that the choice does not drive the conclusion: across
every bandwidth and every feature width we ran, all $154$ pre-registered comparisons
returned no advantage.

The tuning is not perfectly symmetric across the three models, and we state the asymmetry explicitly rather than leave it implicit. The classical RBF baseline and the projected kernel both receive the full $(C, \gamma)$ grid search at every evaluation point, because the projected kernel is by construction an RBF kernel on quantum-projected features and therefore runs through the same tuned classical solver. The fidelity kernel, which is fitted from a precomputed quantum Gram matrix, is trained at a fixed $C = 1$, and the quantity tuned on its behalf is the encoding bandwidth $c$ instead.

The picture that emerges is one of parity in the few-shot regime followed by a classical
win at scale. At its best bandwidth the fidelity kernel is in fact slightly \emph{ahead} of
the tuned classical baseline for every training-set size up to $n = 50$, for example
$0.5507$ against $0.5124$ at $n = 10$ and $0.6518$ against $0.6302$ at $n = 50$. Two of
these gaps reach nominal significance under a paired $t$-test ($p = 0.030$ at $n=5$;
$p = 0.021$ at $n=50$, Wilcoxon $p = 0.008$). None of them, however, satisfies the
pre-registered criterion of Section~\ref{sec:exp3_philosophy}, which requires the lower
bound of the quantum confidence interval to exceed the upper bound of the best classical
one; the intervals overlap substantially at every point. We therefore record these as
parity rather than as an advantage, exactly as the pre-registration was designed to
enforce.

The ordering reverses as data becomes plentiful. At $n = 200$ the classical RBF kernel is
significantly ahead of both quantum kernels ($0.7270$ against $0.7032$ for fidelity,
$p = 0.005$, and $0.6898$ for projected, $p = 0.001$), and it is the only model still
improving appreciably at that point. The projected kernel trails the other two across
almost the whole range despite having the largest geometric difference from the classical
kernel. In all $154$ pre-registered comparisons, spanning every bandwidth and every feature
width, the null hypothesis of a quantum advantage was never rejected, confirming the
absence of a practical advantage for the tested quantum kernels on this dataset.

\subsection{Experiment 4: Quanvolution and Noise Robustness}
\label{sec:exp4_results}

This experiment evaluates the generalisation and intrinsic noise robustness of a hybrid Quanvolutional filter against a strictly parameter-matched classical filter (52 trainable quantum parameters versus 46 classical parameters). To isolate the contribution of the quantum layer from the surrounding architecture, each comparison is run in two regimes: a ``Heavy Head'' configuration (the deep aggregation baseline of Section~\ref{sec:exp4_shared}) and a ``Light Head'' ablation, in which the deep convolutions are removed and the filter outputs route directly into a single linear classifier. All results are reported as the mean over ten random seeds, with significance assessed by a paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test (minimum attainable $p = 2/2^{10} \approx 0.002$).

\subsubsection{Out-of-Distribution and Adversarial Robustness}

Robustness is quantified as the mean accuracy over the full evaluated noise range (the noise-decay AUC normalised by the range); this measure includes the high-noise floor at which both models approach chance, so absolute values are lower than the clean accuracy. Results are summarised in Table~\ref{tab:exp4_auc} and the decay curves in Figure~\ref{fig:exp4_robustness_curves}.

In the Heavy Head configuration the trained quantum and classical filters were statistically indistinguishable. Under OOD noise the quantum filter reached a robustness AUC of \(0.563 \pm 0.014\) against \(0.563 \pm 0.026\) for the classical filter (\(\Delta = +0.000\), \(p = 0.625\)); under Adversarial Training the classical filter was marginally ahead, \(0.675 \pm 0.033\) versus \(0.655 \pm 0.056\) (\(\Delta = -0.020\), \(p = 0.695\)), a difference well within seed variation. Clean accuracy (\(\sigma = 0\)) was similarly close (OOD: \(0.926\) quantum, \(0.914\) classical; Adversarial: \(0.897\) quantum, \(0.910\) classical). The decay trajectories overlap throughout, indicating no standalone quantum advantage when the filter is embedded within a high-capacity classical network.

The Light Head ablation degraded both filters and produced the only statistically significant accuracy difference of the experiment, favouring the classical filter. Under OOD noise the filters remained statistically comparable with no noise. But with added noise the quanvolutional model degraded more steeply, whilethe classical filter held up better.
And even more under Adversarial Training. The classical filter was significantly more robust (quantum \(0.545 \pm 0.034\) versus classical \(0.592 \pm 0.022\), \(\Delta = -0.047\), \(p = 0.014\)). Clean light-head accuracy was \(0.760\) (quantum) versus \(0.786\) (classical) for OOD and \(0.723\) versus \(0.811\) for Adversarial. The quantum light-head configuration also exhibited the largest seed variance in the experiment, with individual seeds collapsing toward chance accuracy. This indicates that the quantum filter's features are less linearly separable than the classical filter's, and rely more heavily on downstream capacity, rather than a uniform failure of the quantum layer.

Training the variational circuit produced no significant benefit in the heavy configuration. Under the Heavy Head the random (frozen) quantum circuit slightly exceeded the trained circuit (OOD AUC \(0.598\) frozen versus \(0.563\) trained; Adversarial \(0.663\) versus \(0.655\)), while under the Light Head training yielded a small uplift (OOD \(0.549\) trained versus \(0.520\) frozen; Adversarial \(0.545\) versus \(0.528\)). None of the frozen-versus-classical comparisons reached significance (all \(p > 0.3\)). The 52 variational parameters therefore contribute little beyond a fixed random feature embedding, particularly when a high-capacity classical head is present.

\subsubsection{Jacobian Smoothness Analysis}

To characterise the structural sensitivity of the trained filters independently of their accuracy, we computed the median Jacobian Frobenius norm \(\|J\|_F\) of each filter's per-patch feature map over sampled clean test patches, evaluated on identical patches for both models. The per-seed distributions are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:exp4_jacobian_dists}.

The smoothness ordering was found to be configuration-dependent. Under the Heavy Head, the trained quantum filter exhibited a significantly lower median Jacobian norm than the classical filter (OOD: \(0.747\) versus \(1.052\), \(p = 0.006\); Adversarial: \(0.684\) versus \(0.938\), \(p = 0.006\)), indicating a smoother, less perturbation-sensitive feature map. Under the Light Head this ordering reversed. The classical filter showed the lower median norm (OOD: \(0.282\) versus \(0.559\); Adversarial: \(0.218\) versus \(0.528\)). Although neither light-head difference reached significance (\(p = 0.106\) and \(p = 0.065\) respectively).

Because the probe is evaluated on the trained filters, whose weights differ between the heavy and light configurations, the measured Jacobian reflects the learned feature map rather than an intrinsic property of the circuit topology. The reversal under ablation therefore does not support a claim of architecture-independent quantum smoothness. We report the heavy-head smoothness as a significant property of the trained model in that regime, while noting that it does not translate into any accuracy or robustness advantage.

\subsubsection{Clean Accuracy and Robustness Outcomes}

Robustness AUC reveals some regime-dependencies:
\begin{itemize}

\subsubsection{Encoding Ablation and Jacobian Smoothness}

The Hybrid QCNN consistently exceeds the pure QCNN in robustness AUC across all four quadrants. Conversely, the quantum maps retain meaningful, relatively smooth operational variance ($0.33$--$0.82$).

\subsubsection{Summary of Findings}

A true QCNN mapping classical spectral data offers no standalone advantage over unconstrained classical networks, as the Huge CNN dominates whenever scaling and adversarial training are permitted. However, at strict parameter parity, the QCNN demonstrates some superiority, particularly in data-starved (Few-Shot) settings where matched classical networks collapse entirely. Furthermore, the QCNN naturally regularizes against overfitting in clean, high-data environments.
