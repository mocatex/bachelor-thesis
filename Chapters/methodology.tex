\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

This section describes the model architectures and the training protocol for the
experiments in this thesis, together with the reasoning behind the design choices.
The experiments share a common discipline. Early trials on our side showed that training a purely
quantum model directly on raw spectra is infeasible given the current hardware and
simulator constraints. Nevertheless, we do evaluate one fully quantum model, a true
Quantum Convolutional Neural Network (QCNN) that embeds the entire spectrum into a
quantum state. All other quantum models are somewhat hybrid, with both a classical and a quantum component. To ensure that the quantum contribution is isolated and measurable, every model—hybrid
or fully quantum—is benchmarked against a classical counterpart built to the same
shape and trained under an identical protocol, so that any observed difference can
be attributed to the contested component. Where the experiments differ is in the
feature stage, the embedding strategy, and the type of quantum processing.

We have experimented with a range of architectures and training protocols, and
the ones described here are the ones that worked best. They are not the only ones we
tried, and they are not necessarily the best possible, but they are the ones that
we settled on after a long process of trial and error. The full implementation is
released as a reproducibility package.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/ShpetimV/QML-AnalyzingGalaxies}}

\subsection{Data Processing Pipeline}
\label{sec:data_pipeline}

To ensure fair and rigorous comparisons across all experiments, every model interacts with the SDSS DR19 dataset through a unified, heavily controlled data pipeline. This pipeline manages memory-efficient ingestion, artifact mitigation, robust augmentation, and the strict isolation of spectral features.

\subsubsection{Data Ingestion and Memory Management}
The raw dataset spans millions of astronomical observations, posing a substantial memory bottleneck. To circumvent this, the pipeline employs zero-copy datasets and a streamed ingestion process. Using the \texttt{pyarrow} backend, the parquet files are parsed in continuous batches and mapped directly into pre-allocated NumPy arrays. This approach prevents the catastrophic RAM spikes associated with loading the full dataset simultaneously. When an experiment demands a specific subset of the data (such as the binary classification in \hyperref[sec:exp1_methods]{Experiment 1}), the pipeline dynamically filters the dataset to the requested classes and remaps their integer labels, enabling a seamless transition between the full 62-class problem and targeted sub-problems.

\subsubsection{Preprocessing and Artifact Mitigation}
All SDSS spectra are delivered on the same fixed logarithmic wavelength grid with a
constant step of $\Delta \log_{10} \lambda = 10^{-4}$~\cite{smee2013spectrographs}, so
every observation in our dataset consists of exactly 4648 flux bins spanning
$3567$--$10399$\,\AA. The logarithmic spacing means the bin width in wavelength grows
across the range, from $0.82$\,\AA{} per bin at the blue end to $2.40$\,\AA{} per bin at
the red end. Flux is reported in units of
$10^{-17}\,\mathrm{erg}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\,\mathrm{cm}^{-2}\,\mathrm{\AA}^{-1}$. Across our
curated subset the bulk of the flux values is of order tens to hundreds in these units:
the median bin value is $\approx 57$, the first-to-ninety-ninth percentile range is
$-1.4$ to $2.5 \times 10^{3}$, and a typical single spectrum spans roughly $-7$ to $160$
with a standard deviation of $\approx 27$.

The raw distribution has a heavy tail that is not physical. About $0.25\%$ of all bins
exceed $|f| > 10^{5}$, reaching magnitudes of up to $10^{9}$, and we measured that
$99.9\%$ of these extreme bins lie within the first or last 100 bins of the grid. These
are spectrograph edge artifacts at the boundary of the instrument's sensitivity, where
the calibration divides by a vanishing throughput. If left untreated, the networks fixate
on these non-physical anomalies, since they dominate any per-spectrum normalisation. We
therefore crop the first and last 100 wavelength bins of every spectrum prior to any
further processing, which removes the artifacts while discarding only the extreme blue
and extreme red ends of the range.

Following the crop, each individual spectrum is strictly standard-scaled (zero mean, unit
variance) to normalize flux intensities. Because the sequence length must remain uniform
for batched tensor operations, the resulting 4448 bins are finally truncated to the fixed
length of 4096 used by all models. In practice the pipeline therefore only ever truncates:
it discards the 352 reddest bins, $7.9\%$ of the cropped spectrum, corresponding to the
region beyond $\approx 9370$\,\AA{} where the instrument response is weakest. The
zero-padding branch of the pipeline exists for generality but is never triggered on this
dataset, where all spectra share the same grid.

Optionally, the dataset provides a vector of auxiliary scalar features, most notably the measured redshift. However, our exploratory trials revealed that these scalars acted as a data-leaking crutch; the networks exploited the redshift values to trivially separate classes, achieving artificially high accuracies without actually learning structural representations from the flux spectra. To enforce genuine spectral learning, these auxiliary vectors are explicitly withheld from the pipeline, forcing all models to derive their predictions exclusively from the raw 1D flux.

\subsubsection{Physically Motivated Data Augmentation}
\label{sec:augmentation}
An on-the-fly augmentation pipeline is applied exclusively during the training phase. The
transformations are drawn stochastically each time a spectrum is loaded, so a model never
sees the same realisation of a training spectrum twice and the effective size of the
training set grows with the number of epochs. Augmentation of this kind is a standard
regulariser: it enlarges the region of input space covered by the training distribution
and thereby reduces the gap between training and validation performance
\cite{shorten2019augmentation}. That gap is the concrete symptom we target here; the
baseline of Section~\ref{sec:baseline_cnn} still ends training with a noticeably higher
training than validation accuracy even with the full pipeline enabled.

Four of the six transformations below are chosen so that the perturbed spectrum remains a
plausible observation of the same object, and we state the physical quantity each one
stands in for together with the magnitude actually used. The remaining two are generic
machine-learning regularisers with no astrophysical interpretation, and we label them as
such rather than dressing them up as physics.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Class-Aware Redshift Shifting:} Because the wavelength grid is
          logarithmic, a redshift is exactly a translation along the bin axis:
          $\log_{10}\lambda_\text{obs} = \log_{10}\lambda_\text{emit} + \log_{10}(1+z)$,
          so a shift of $k$ bins corresponds to $\log_{10}(1+z) = k \cdot 10^{-4}$. This is
          the same equivalence the SDSS classification pipeline exploits when it slides
          templates over a redshift grid in $\log \lambda$~\cite{bolton2012boss}. We
          therefore roll the spectrum by a small integer number of bins, which mimics a
          line-of-sight velocity offset of $\approx 69\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ per bin. The
          maximum shift is class-dependent: one bin for stars
          ($\approx 69\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, the scale of Galactic stellar radial
          velocities) and five bins for galaxies and quasars
          ($\approx 345\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, the scale of peculiar velocities and of the
          residual uncertainty on a measured redshift). The shift is deliberately kept
          this small so that the augmented spectrum stays consistent with the class label.
    \item \textit{Gaussian Noise:} Random standard normal noise scaled to $0.5\%$ of the
          standard deviation of the specific spectrum is injected. SDSS spectra are taken
          under widely varying conditions and the per-fibre signal-to-noise ratio varies
          accordingly~\cite{smee2013spectrographs}, so this represents the same object
          observed under slightly poorer conditions rather than a specific noise source.
    \item \textit{Flux Scaling:} The amplitude of the spectrum is multiplied by a random
          factor drawn uniformly from $[0.9, 1.1]$. We note explicitly that this is
          \emph{not} a model of distance or apparent magnitude: the per-spectrum standard
          scaling described above has already removed the absolute flux level, so no
          information about brightness survives to be perturbed. The transformation is
          applied after normalisation and acts as a $\pm 10\%$ amplitude jitter that
          prevents the network from keying on the exact post-normalisation scale.
    \item \textit{Contiguous Masking:} One or two random contiguous chunks, each 15 to 50
          bins wide, are zeroed out. Methodologically this is SpecAugment-style time-frequency
          masking transferred from speech spectrograms~\cite{park2019specaugment}. It also
          has a direct observational counterpart: real SDSS spectra contain masked pixels,
          cosmic-ray hits and residuals from strong sky emission lines such as
          $\lambda 5577$ and $\lambda 6300$, so a usable classifier must tolerate missing
          windows. At $0.82$--$2.40$\,\AA{} per bin a masked chunk spans roughly
          $12$--$120$\,\AA, which is wide enough to remove an entire emission line. Since
          emission and absorption lines are the primary discriminants in spectral
          classification~\cite{gray2005photospheres, baldwin1981bpt}, deleting them forces
          the feature extractor to also use the continuum rather than keying on a single
          strong line.
    \item \textit{Gaussian Smoothing:} A 1D Gaussian blur with $\sigma$ drawn uniformly
          from $[0,0.5]$ bins is applied. This degrades the effective line-spread function
          of the spectrum, that is, it emulates the same object observed at slightly lower
          spectral resolution~\cite{smee2013spectrographs}. The kernel is deliberately
          narrow, at most a few bins wide, so that line profiles are broadened but not
          erased.
    \item \textit{Mixup:} With $50\%$ probability an input is linearly interpolated with
          another random sample (\(\lambda \sim \text{Beta}(0.4,0.4)\)) and the loss becomes
          the same weighted combination of the two labels' cross-entropy terms, so training
          uses soft mixed targets instead of hard class labels~\cite{zhang2018mixup}. A
          blend of two spectra is not itself a physically realisable observation. We include
          it purely as a regulariser on the decision boundary and make no astrophysical
          claim for it.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Addressing Class Imbalance}
\label{sec:imbalance}
The curated SDSS DR19 subset is highly imbalanced. Class sizes range from 276 spectra for
the rarest class (\texttt{STAR\_M0}) up to the acquisition ceiling of 25{,}000 spectra for
the most common ones, a ratio of roughly 90 to 1. Left unaddressed, a model minimises its
average loss by learning the majority classes and ignoring the minority ones, which
produces a respectable overall accuracy together with near-zero recall on exactly the rare
classes this thesis is interested in. The pipeline therefore implements two independent
countermeasures, and the experiments select between them.

The first is a \emph{weighted random sampler}. Each sample is assigned a weight inversely
proportional to the size of its class, and training batches are then drawn with
replacement according to those weights, so every class is expected to appear equally often
per epoch. The second is a \emph{focal loss}~\cite{lin2018focalloss} with focusing
parameter $\gamma = 2.0$ and additional inverse-frequency class weights, which leaves the
sampling untouched and instead down-weights the loss contribution of examples the model
already classifies confidently.

The two are alternatives rather than complements, since combining them would correct the
same imbalance twice. As reported in Section~\ref{sec:baseline_training}, the sampler
proved unstable in practice and all reported models use the focal loss.

\subsection{Baseline: Big Classical CNN}
\label{sec:baseline_cnn}

\subsubsection{Design Principles}
\label{sec:baseline_philosophy}

The baseline architecture, which we nickname the \emph{Beast} (our own informal label for
it, not an established term), is designed to solve the full 62-class spectral
classification problem on the SDSS DR19 dataset and is later repurposed as the frozen
extractor in \hyperref[sec:exp2_methods]{Experiment 2}. Its complete structure is shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_cnn}.

The design follows from the structure of the input. An optical spectrum superposes two
very different kinds of signal: a broad, slowly varying continuum set by the thermal
emission and reddening of the source, and narrow emission and absorption features whose
depths, widths and positions carry most of the classification
information~\cite{gray2005photospheres}. The SDSS classification pipeline itself reflects
this split, fitting spectra as combinations of smooth continuum components and line
templates~\cite{bolton2012boss}. For the 62-class subset used here, separating the classes
therefore requires both fine-grained local feature extraction, at the scale of a few
wavelength bins, and a receptive field wide enough to see the continuum shape across the
full range. We do not claim this as a statement about arbitrary spectroscopic surveys, only
about the data actually used in this thesis.

To achieve this, the architecture combines three distinct paradigms. First, a multi-scale convolutional stem captures features at varying resolutions simultaneously \cite{szegedy2015going}. Second, a deep backbone of one-dimensional Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) residual blocks \cite{hu2019squeeze} with dilated convolutions \cite{yu2016dilated} extracts hierarchical features while dynamically weighting the most informative spectral channels. Finally, a Transformer encoder \cite{vaswani2017attention} acts on the resulting sequence to capture long-range dependencies between distant spectral lines that would otherwise require an impractically deep purely convolutional network.

To prevent the dead-neuron collapse observed in early trials with ReLU activations, the entire network relies on the Gaussian Error Linear Unit (GELU) \cite{hendrycks2016gaussian}, ensuring smoother gradient flow throughout the deep pipeline.

\textbf{Hyperparameters.} We should be explicit that the remaining architectural
constants were not tuned. The two Transformer layers, the eight attention heads, the
feed-forward width of 2048, the OneCycle peak learning rate of $10^{-3}$ and the weight
decay of $10^{-4}$ are conventional defaults for networks of this shape, chosen by hand
and adjusted only where a training run visibly failed. No systematic hyperparameter
search was run over them, for the practical reason that a single 300-epoch run on the
full 62-class dataset is expensive and the baseline's role here is to be a strong and
honestly trained reference rather than an optimum. The consequence is that the reported
$84.5\%$ should be read as a solid achievable accuracy for this architecture rather than
as its ceiling, which if anything makes the classical side of every later comparison
conservative.

\textbf{Provenance.} The Beast is our own design and we make no claim that it is a novel
astrophysical architecture. It is assembled entirely from established components, the
multi-scale stem of Inception-style networks~\cite{szegedy2015going}, Squeeze-and-Excitation
residual blocks~\cite{hu2019squeeze}, dilated convolutions~\cite{yu2016dilated} and a
Transformer encoder~\cite{vaswani2017attention}, and was arrived at by iterative refinement
against validation accuracy rather than derived from a published astronomical model. The
closest published references for this task are GaSNet-II~\cite{zhong2024gasnet}, a deep
network for joint SDSS spectral classification and redshift estimation, and the
convolutional stellar classifier of Sharma et al.~\cite{sharma2020cnn}; to our knowledge no
published architecture targets a 62-class SDSS subclass problem, which is why no direct
architectural template was available. Rather than argue for the design on paper, we validate
it empirically: trained on the 13-class subset of GaSNet-II, the same network reaches
$92.51\%$ accuracy (Section~\ref{sec:gasnet_replica} reports the replication), which places
our classical side at the level of the published state of the art on a comparable task. The
purpose of the Beast in this thesis is to be a strong and honestly tuned classical reference
point for the quantum comparisons, not a contribution to spectral classification in its own
right.

\subsubsection{Architecture Pipeline}
\label{sec:baseline_arch}

The model accepts a 1D flux spectrum of 4096 bins as input. This number was chosen since
its power-of-two structure allows for efficient downsampling and also for a straightforward
mapping to quantum circuits in later experiments. As described in
Section~\ref{sec:data_pipeline}, the 4648-bin SDSS grid is cropped to 4448 bins and then
truncated to this length, so the model sees the range from $\approx 3650$ to
$\approx 9370$\,\AA{} at $0.82$--$2.40$\,\AA{} per bin.
The processing pipeline is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_cnn}.

\textbf{Multi-Scale Stem:} The input is first passed through parallel 1D convolutions with
kernel sizes of 5, 15, and 31. This multi-branch approach allows the network to
simultaneously "see" narrow spectral anomalies and wider bands. Two considerations fix
these particular values. First, the sizes are odd rather than the powers of two used
elsewhere in the architecture, because an odd kernel with symmetric padding keeps the
receptive field centred on a bin; an even kernel introduces a half-bin offset between input
and output, which is undesirable when the position of a line centroid is itself part of the
signal. Second, the three widths were picked to cover the physical scales present in the
data in roughly geometric steps. At the sampling of the SDSS grid, a kernel of 5 bins spans
$4$--$12$\,\AA, the width of a narrow, barely resolved emission or absorption line; 15 bins
spans $12$--$36$\,\AA; and 31 bins spans $25$--$75$\,\AA, which is the scale of a broad
quasar emission line (a few thousand $\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ corresponds to roughly
$50$\,\AA{} at $5000$\,\AA) or of a molecular band in a cool star. The values are thus the
odd numbers nearest to $4$, $16$ and $32$ that also match these physical widths. The
outputs are concatenated along the channel dimension, normalized, activated, and pooled to
form the initial feature map.

\textbf{SE-Residual Bottleneck Backbone:} The core of the network consists of five stages of \texttt{SEResidualBottleneck1D} blocks, progressively expanding the channel dimension from 64 up to 1024 while downsampling the spatial (wavelength) dimension. To expand the receptive field without an explosion in parameter count, later blocks within Stages 2, 3, and 4 utilize dilated convolutions (up to a dilation rate of 4). Every block incorporates a Squeeze-and-Excitation module, which applies global average pooling to the block's output and uses a two-layer bottleneck to learn a channel-wise gating multiplier, forcing the network to amplify relevant feature maps and suppress noise.

\textbf{Attention Mechanism:} After the convolutional stages, the sequence is permeated
with learnable positional embeddings and fed into a two-layer Transformer encoder with 8
attention heads and a hidden dimension of 2048. The intent of this stage is to let the
model relate features that are far apart in wavelength at constant depth. Such relations
are known to be diagnostic in practice: the standard emission-line classification of
extragalactic objects rests on ratios of lines separated by thousands of \AA{}, for example
$[\mathrm{O\,\textsc{iii}}]\,\lambda 5007/\mathrm{H}\beta$ against
$[\mathrm{N\,\textsc{ii}}]\,\lambda 6584/\mathrm{H}\alpha$ in the Baldwin--Phillips--Terlevich
diagram~\cite{baldwin1981bpt, kewley2001starburst}, which separates star-forming galaxies
from active galactic nuclei. A purely convolutional stack can only reach such a pairing by
stacking enough layers for the receptive fields to overlap, whereas attention couples the
two positions directly. We state this as the motivation for including the encoder rather
than as a verified property of the trained model: we did not run an ablation isolating the
Transformer, so we cannot claim that the network has in fact learned these specific line
pairings. The gradient-based interpretability analysis in
Section~\ref{sec:interpretability} offers only indirect and partial support, since it
probes the smaller extractor of Experiment 1 rather than this baseline, but it does show
that the learned attribution concentrates on broad, class-dependent emission regions rather
than on individual bins.

\textbf{Classification Head:} The sequence is collapsed into a single 1024-dimensional vector via adaptive average pooling. If auxiliary features are provided, they are concatenated here. A deep Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) head maps these representations down through hidden layers projecting them to the 62 class logits.

\subsubsection{Training Protocol and Sub-Problem Identification}
\label{sec:baseline_training}

The model was trained from scratch on the full SDSS DR19 dataset. We optimized the training further by using a OneCycle learning rate \cite{smith2017onecyclelr} schedule with a maximum learning rate of $10^{-3}$ and a weight decay of $10^{-4}$. This uses a warm-up phase to ramp up the learning rate, followed by a gradual cooldown, which helps the model converge faster and escape sharp minima. Because the schedule is defined over a fixed horizon, early stopping would have cut it off mid-cooldown and would have been counterproductive, so the model was trained for a fixed 300 epochs.

Of the two remedies for class imbalance described in Section~\ref{sec:imbalance}, the
baseline uses the focal loss. We first experimented with the weighted random sampler, but
oversampling the rarest class, with 276 spectra against up to 25{,}000 in the most common
ones, meant the same few hundred spectra recurred many times per epoch; this destabilised
training and degraded overall performance. The focal loss
\cite{lin2018focalloss} instead leaves the sampling untouched and reshapes the objective,
down-weighting examples the model already classifies confidently, which are predominantly
those of the majority classes, and concentrating the gradient on the harder,
underrepresented ones. This approach led to more stable training and improved performance
on the minority classes.

Beyond serving as the frozen backbone for \hyperref[sec:exp2_methods]{Experiment 2}, the baseline plays a crucial diagnostic role. During evaluation, a custom metric tracker generates a row-normalized confusion matrix to compute per-class recall. More importantly, it performs an automated algorithmic analysis to extract two patterns for which we adopted our own informal shorthand: \emph{Mirrors}, meaning symmetric confusion pairs where the CNN frequently mistakes class A for class B and vice versa, and \emph{Black Holes}, meaning sink classes that absorb a large number of false positives from their neighbours. The second label is a metaphor for the shape of the confusion matrix column and has no connection to astrophysical black holes, which are not among the classes considered in this thesis. This systemic failure analysis provides the empirical justification for the classes selected in the following experiments; by isolating the exact pairs that the highly parameterized classical Beast struggles to separate, we define the hardest possible target for the subsequent quantum head.

\subsection{Experiment 1: Binary Classification}
\label{sec:exp1_methods}

\subsubsection{Design Principles}
\label{sec:exp1_philosophy}

The first experiment keeps the setup as simple as possible. The baseline
(Section~\ref{sec:baseline_cnn}) confirmed that the spectra are learnable. Its 62-class
confusion matrix (Figure~\ref{fig:cnn_baseline_confusion_matrix} in
Appendix~\ref{app:baseline_confusion}) identified the single hardest pair of classes,
\texttt{STAR\_BROWN\_DWARF\_L} against \texttt{STAR\_M8}, whose mean spectra are nearly
identical (Figure~\ref{fig:ml_similarity}). The coarse star-galaxy-quasar task, by
contrast, is essentially solved (Section~\ref{sec:related}), so the interesting question
lies at these fine-grained boundaries. We therefore test a deliberately light quantum
head on exactly this hardest pair.

Two constraints shaped the design. A flux spectrum has roughly four thousand bins, far
more than a small quantum circuit of at most eight qubits can ingest, so a classical
feature extractor in front is unavoidable. To keep the comparison fair, we build two
models that are identical everywhere except for one block in the middle. Both reuse the
same feature extractor at the front and the same classifier head at the back. The first
model, which we call the \emph{quantum model}, uses a small variational quantum circuit
(VQC) as this middle block. The second, which we call the \emph{classical mirror},
replaces that circuit with a parameter-matched classical block of ordinary neural-network
layers. Because the two models are otherwise identical, any difference in accuracy
between them can only come from the middle block.

\subsubsection{Shared Architecture}
\label{sec:exp1_arch}

Both models share the pipeline of Figure~\ref{fig:exp1_pipeline}. A trainable
one-dimensional CNN, the \texttt{SpectralFeatureExtractor}, compresses the 4096-bin
spectrum to a small feature vector whose width equals the number of qubits. The
features are bounded to $[-\pi,\pi]$ by a $\tanh(\cdot)\,\pi$ transform, the middle
stage processes them, and a small MLP head of \texttt{Linear} to width 32, batch
normalisation, a ReLU, dropout and a final \texttt{Linear} to two logits produces
the prediction. We fix a notation convention here that is used in every circuit diagram
of this thesis: an unaccented symbol such as $x_q$ denotes a raw feature, while the
accented $\tilde{x}_q$ denotes the same feature after it has been squashed into a bounded
angular domain and loaded onto qubit $q$. Only bounded values are ever fed to a circuit, so
the inputs of all our feature maps carry the tilde, whether the bound comes from the
$\tanh(\cdot)\,\pi$ transform used here and in \hyperref[sec:exp2_methods]{Experiment 2} or
from the $\tfrac{\pi}{2}\tanh(\cdot)$ squashing of
\hyperref[sec:exp3_methods]{Experiment 3}. This extractor is our own design, following standard one-dimensional CNN practice for
spectra rather than adapted from a specific reference. It is a compact network specific
to this experiment, distinct from the 14-million-parameter baseline of
Section~\ref{sec:baseline_cnn} (Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_cnn}). Unlike the frozen
backbone of \hyperref[sec:exp2_methods]{Experiment 2}, it is trained jointly with the
rest of the model. Its internal structure is given in
Figure~\ref{fig:cnn_extractor}, with three convolutional blocks growing the channels
from 1 to 64, an adaptive average pool and a three-stage projection head down to the
feature width. The extractor holds about 24000 of the roughly 24500 trainable
parameters, so the contested middle stage is a tiny fraction of the whole model.

\subsubsection{Quantum and Classical Middle Stages}
\label{sec:exp1_middle}

The quantum middle is the data-reuploading VQC of Figure~\ref{fig:binary_vqc}. Each
layer re-uploads the bounded features with $R_Y$ rotations, applies a trainable general
single-qubit rotation $\mathrm{Rot}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma) = R_Z(\gamma)\,R_Y(\beta)\,R_Z(\alpha)$
on every qubit and entangles the register with a ring of CNOT gates, after which the
qubits are read out as Pauli-$Z$ expectations. Because the trainable rotation is a
general single-qubit rotation rather than a second $R_Y$, it does not commute with the
$R_Y$ data upload. Successive layers therefore do not merge into a single rotation. The
re-uploading realises a genuine multi-frequency function of the input rather than
collapsing to one rotation. The CNOT ring provides entanglement across qubits for the
same reason. We use angle encoding with re-uploading rather than amplitude encoding. The
re-uploading structure is what lets us sweep the qubit count against depth at a fixed
parameter budget and control the accessible frequencies, whereas amplitude encoding would
fix the register at only a couple of qubits and remove that freedom. We tried amplitude
encoding in early experiments and saw no benefit. The amplitude-versus-angle question is
examined more systematically in \hyperref[sec:exp5_methods]{Experiment 5}, where a
directly amplitude-encoded circuit proves markedly more fragile.
The classical mirror replaces this circuit with the block of
Figure~\ref{fig:classical_layer}, a \texttt{Linear} expansion to width eight, a
$\tanh$ and a \texttt{Linear} back to the feature width. The $\tanh$ is deliberate,
because an earlier ReLU version collapsed onto the majority class through the same
dead-ReLU failure later confirmed in \hyperref[sec:exp2_methods]{Experiment 2}, so the honest control uses the non-dying activation. With four features the classical block holds 76 parameters
against the 72 of the VQC, so the two middle stages are matched to within a handful
of parameters.

\subsubsection{Sweep and Training Protocol}
\label{sec:exp1_sweep}

Rather than commit to one circuit we swept the geometry. Five quantum shapes were
run from narrow and deep to wide and shallow, namely 2 qubits with 12 layers, 3 with
8, 4 with 6, 6 with 4 and 8 with 3. Every shape has the same
$n_\text{qubits}\cdot n_\text{layers}\cdot 3 = 72$ trainable angles, so the sweep
trades qubit count against circuit depth at a constant number of trainable angles. The
classical mirror was swept over two bottleneck widths of four and eight features.
Each configuration was trained with the Adam optimiser at a fixed learning rate for
a fixed 30 epochs and repeated over three seeds, and we report the mean and standard
deviation across seeds. The protocol was kept deliberately plain, since the aim was
a controlled comparison rather than a tuned performance record.

\subsection{Experiment 2: Classifier Heads on a Frozen Extractor}
\label{sec:exp2_methods}

\subsubsection{Design Principles}
\label{sec:exp2_philosophy}

Experiment 2 isolates the head. The task is a four-class problem built from the four
hardest classes in the baseline confusion matrix
(Appendix~\ref{app:baseline_confusion}), so the head is tested where the classical Beast
already struggles. We reuse the pretrained Beast as a frozen feature extractor and vary
only the small head on top of it, so feature learning is removed from the comparison and
every measured difference comes from the head alone. To compare a
quantum head against a classical one fairly, we do not try to argue that a rotation angle
and a network weight carry the same capacity. Instead we hold the whole pipeline fixed
and swap only the internals of the head, building a classical control with the same
parameter count and the same pipeline shape as the quantum model. Any difference in
accuracy is then attributable to the head, not to the units we happen to count. We also
report more than final accuracy. Following standard practice, we track convergence speed,
training stability and computational cost, since parameter count alone is a narrow lens.
The four heads introduced below each isolate a specific question.

\subsubsection{Frozen Feature Extractor (the Beast)}
\label{sec:beast}

The shared backbone is the same network we nickname the Beast in
Section~\ref{sec:baseline_cnn}, the 14-million-parameter CNN-Transformer used as the
baseline (Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_cnn}),
trained from scratch on the full 62-class problem where it reaches roughly 84\% accuracy.
We do not repeat its architecture here. For Experiment 2 its final
\texttt{Linear(128, num\_classes)} layer is replaced by an identity map and every
parameter is frozen, so the backbone becomes a fixed function that turns each
spectrum into the same 128-dimensional feature vector. We freeze it rather than
fine-tune it for two reasons. First, a 14-million-parameter backbone trained jointly
with a tiny head could absorb or mask the behaviour of that head and confound the
comparison, whereas a fixed backbone presents every head with identical inputs.
Second, simulating the quantum circuit is expensive. Reusing a pretrained extractor
avoids repeatedly backpropagating through both the backbone and the circuit. The backbone is also kept permanently in evaluation mode so that its
batch-normalisation running statistics do not drift while the head is trained.

\subsubsection{Classification Heads}
\label{sec:heads}

The basic idea is simple. We keep the whole pipeline fixed and compare four small heads
on top of the frozen extractor, each isolating one question. Every head maps the
128-dimensional features to four class logits. Model~A is a comfortably sized classical
head and Model~B is the hybrid quantum head. Models~C and~D are the parameter-matched
classical controls for Model~B.

The \emph{dense classical head} (Model~A) is a two-layer perceptron,
\texttt{Linear(128,38)} $\to$ ReLU $\to$ Dropout$(0.2)$ $\to$ \texttt{Linear(38,4)},
with $128\cdot 38 + 38 = 4902$ and $38\cdot 4 + 4 = 156$ weights, for $5058$
trainable parameters. It is the conventional, comfortably sized baseline and answers
the question of what accuracy is reachable when the head is not parameter-starved.
Its full pipeline, the frozen extractor followed by the two-layer head, is shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:densehead}.

The \emph{hybrid quantum head} (Model~B) first compresses the features with a
trainable \texttt{Linear(128,4)} bottleneck of $516$ parameters, squashes the four
outputs into $[-\pi,\pi]$ with a $\tanh(\cdot)\,\pi$ transform and feeds them to the
variational quantum circuit of Section~\ref{sec:vqc} and Figure~\ref{fig:vqc}. The full
chain therefore reduces the four-thousand-bin spectrum to $128$ features, then to four
numbers, then to a four-qubit circuit, so the number of qubits is $n=4$. We use four
qubits because the task has four classes and the circuit reads out one qubit per class.
The four Pauli-$Z$ expectations returned by the circuit pass through a trainable
\texttt{Linear(4,4)} readout of $20$ parameters, drawn as the final stage of
Figure~\ref{fig:modelb}, which with the $20$ circuit angles
gives $556$ trainable parameters. Two choices in this head are deliberate. The
bottleneck is trainable rather than fixed because the circuit accepts only four
numbers, so gradient descent can then discover the four discriminative directions
the circuit needs instead of being handed an arbitrary projection. The readout is a
trainable linear layer because the raw expectation values are confined to $[-1,1]$
and tie qubit $i$ to class $i$, so passing them through a learned map lets the logits
leave that range and removes the artificial cap on softmax confidence. The complete
pipeline of this head, from the input spectrum through to the class logits, is shown
in Figure~\ref{fig:modelb}.

The \emph{parameter-matched classical controls} (Models~C and~D) put this fairness
idea into practice. They are structural mirrors of the hybrid head in which the quantum
circuit is replaced by a classical block of exactly the same parameter count. Both use a \texttt{Linear(128,4)} bottleneck followed by a $\tanh$, which
reproduces the bounded input that the circuit receives, then a middle
\texttt{Linear(4,4)} that stands in for the circuit and a \texttt{Linear(4,4)}
readout, again $516 + 20 + 20 = 556$ trainable parameters. Matching the count by
construction keeps the comparison fair without appealing to any claim about angles and
weights being equivalent units. Model~C places a ReLU between the two width-4 layers and
Model~D places a
$\tanh$ there instead. This single controlled change exists to test whether any
behaviour attributed to the quantum circuit might instead be an optimisation artefact
of the surrounding classical structure, a question taken up in the results. The three
interchangeable middle stages that distinguish Models~B, C and~D are compared in
Figure~\ref{fig:middles}, with every other stage of the pipeline in
Figure~\ref{fig:modelb} held identical.

\subsubsection{Variational Quantum Circuit}
\label{sec:vqc}

The quantum head maps the four real values produced by the trainable bottleneck into
the circuit shown in Figure~\ref{fig:vqc}. The four numbers, already squashed into
$[-\pi,\pi]$, are used as rotation angles. Each layer re-uploads these inputs with
$R_Y$ rotations, applies a trainable $R_Y(\theta^{(\ell)}_q)$ rotation on every qubit
and entangles the register with a ring of CNOT gates. After $L=5$ layers each qubit
is measured in the Pauli-$Z$ basis. The four expectation values feed the trainable
readout. Unlike the three-angle rotations of the binary circuit in Experiment 1
(Figure~\ref{fig:binary_vqc}), this head uses a single trainable $R_Y$ angle per qubit
per layer. The reason is the parameter budget. Experiment 1 swept the circuit geometry at
a fixed budget of $72$ angles, whereas here the circuit must fit inside the
$556$-parameter head that the classical controls match, so we use the smallest circuit
that can still express the task. Data re-uploading, the re-injection of the input in every
layer, lets a shallow circuit represent higher-order functions of its input that a single
encoding layer cannot reach. The trainable $R_Y(\theta)$ shares the axis of the $R_Y(x)$
upload, so on a single qubit the two would merge into one rotation. The ring of CNOT gates
between layers prevents this. It entangles the qubits so the circuit is not a product of
independent single-qubit rotations and does not collapse. It also spreads correlations
across all four qubits at a depth that grows only linearly with their number. An
exploratory variant with full three-axis rotations added parameters without improving
accuracy.

    \caption{Variational quantum circuit of the quantum head (Model~B). The four qubits
        start in $|0\rangle$. Within each layer the bounded bottleneck outputs $\tilde{x}_q$
        are re-uploaded
        through $R_Y(\tilde{x}_q)$ rotations, followed by trainable $R_Y(\theta^{(\ell)}_q)$

        final Pauli-$Z$ measurement on each qubit yields four expectation values that are
        passed to a trainable linear readout. The $20$ trainable circuit angles are the

    \label{fig:vqc}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Encoding Variants for the Ablation}
\label{sec:encoding}

Model~B learns its encoding end-to-end through the trainable bottleneck, so its accuracy
could come from the quantum circuit or from that learned compression. This ablation
separates the two. We hold the four-qubit circuit and readout fixed and change only how
the four input numbers are produced, replacing the learned bottleneck with frozen
alternatives.
A \texttt{FrozenPCABottleneck} fits a principal-component projection once on the
training-set features and then keeps it fixed, so it is informative but not learnable.
The \emph{pure-PCA} variant feeds a frozen \texttt{PCA(128,4)} directly into the same
circuit and readout, leaving only the $20$ circuit angles and the $20$ readout weights
trainable, about $40$ parameters in total. A \emph{PCA-hybrid} variant inserts a small
trainable \texttt{Linear(64,4)} after a frozen \texttt{PCA(128,64)}, for roughly $300$
trainable parameters. Both are compared against the learned \texttt{Linear(128,4)} of
Model~B, which encodes into the same four qubits but chooses its directions
end-to-end through the loss. Because the qubit count is held at four throughout, any
difference between these variants must come from the encoding rather than from the
size of the circuit.

\subsubsection{Training Protocol}
\label{sec:training}

Experiment 2 uses a four-class subset balanced to 1000 samples per class and split
$70/15/15$ into train, validation and test with seed $42$. All heads are trained on
top of the frozen extractor with the Adam optimiser at a learning rate of $10^{-3}$
and weight decay $10^{-4}$, a batch size of $256$ and a cross-entropy loss, for 100
epochs. The PCA-based variants fit their projection on the training features once
before optimisation begins. The extractor remains frozen and in evaluation mode
throughout. In line with the design principles we record not only the final test
accuracy but also per-class recall, the test confusion matrices, the convergence
behaviour and the training cost.

\subsection{Experiment 3: Few-Shot Classification with Quantum Kernels}
\label{sec:exp3_methods}

\subsubsection{Design Principles}
\label{sec:exp3_philosophy}

This experiment pivots from the highly parameterized neural networks of the earlier experiments to the few-shot learning regime, investigating whether quantum support vector machines (QSVMs) exhibit a trainable advantage when data is exceedingly scarce. Deep classical networks are notoriously prone to overfitting on small datasets. Quantum kernels, mapping data into an exponentially large Hilbert space, theoretically offer enhanced linear separability. However, evaluating a quantum advantage requires a stringent protocol.

To ensure a defensible comparison, this experiment operates under three constraints. First, we enforce strict balanced subsetting to prevent silent majority-class collapse at small sample sizes. Second, we evaluate over multiple resampled seeds per subset size, reporting the mean accuracy with 95\% confidence intervals rather than relying on a single favourable data draw. Third, the classical baseline, an RBF kernel SVM, is hyperparameter-tuned with the same rigour as the quantum models; a quantum model outperforming an untuned classical model does not constitute a true quantum advantage. Finally, we establish a pre-registered criterion for advantage: a quantum model is deemed superior at a specific sample size $n$ only if the lower bound of its 95\% confidence interval strictly exceeds the upper bound of the best classical model's confidence interval.

\subsubsection{Data Processing and Bounded Encoding}
\label{sec:exp3_encoding}

For this experiment, 4 hard-to-separate classes were identified from the baseline's confusion matrix, via a four-crossing analysis that isolated the most symmetric and entangled pairs. Initial trials used easy-to-separate classes, but the resulting near-perfect accuracies at small $n$ made it impossible to detect any advantage.

Unlike the frozen convolutional extractor in \hyperref[sec:exp2_methods]{Experiment 2}, the
QSVM pipeline avoids high-capacity trainable feature extraction to prevent classical
parameters from masking the quantum kernel's contribution. Instead, the raw 4096-bin flux
spectra are deterministically downsampled using 1D adaptive average pooling to
$N_{\text{features}}$ bins, with one pooled bin loaded onto each of the
$N_{\text{features}}$ qubits of the quantum circuit. The reduction is therefore a fixed,
parameter-free average over contiguous wavelength windows, not a learned projection.

We swept $N_{\text{features}}$ up to 16 to determine the optimal dimensionality for both
classical and quantum state preparation, and settled on $N_{\text{features}} = 12$ for all
reported runs. The full 4096-bin spectrum is thus compressed to 12 numbers, each the mean
of roughly 341 consecutive bins ($\approx 480$\,\AA{} of the spectrum), and encoded into a
register of exactly 12 qubits. Above this width the classification metrics plateaued while
the cost of simulating the register kept growing, and below it the pooled representation
became too coarse to separate the classes; the sweep supporting this choice is reported in
Section~\ref{sec:exp3_results}.

A known failure mode in quantum kernels acting on raw continuous data is $2\pi$ wrap-around \cite{Bosco2025Periodic}, where distinct scalar values collapse onto the same rotation angle, and exponential kernel concentration \cite{thanasilp2024Exponential}, where highly expressive feature maps converge to a near-identity Gram matrix. To mitigate this, the pooled features $x_i$ are explicitly standard-scaled and subsequently mapped into a strictly bounded angular domain using a squashing function:
$$ \tilde{x}_q = \frac{\pi}{2} \tanh(c \cdot x_q) $$
where $x_q$ is the pooled feature destined for qubit $q$ and $c$ is a continuous bandwidth
scaling parameter. Following the notation convention of Section~\ref{sec:exp1_arch}, the
tilde marks the bounded value that is actually loaded into the circuit. This ensures all
encoded rotations remain well within a single period $[-\pi/2, \pi/2]$, minimizing
wrap-around and artificially induced concentration.

The bounded features $\tilde{x}_q$ are injected into the quantum state via the feature map
$U(x)$ illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:qsvm_circuit}. The mapping consists of $R_Y$ and
$R_Z$ rotations encoding each feature onto its respective qubit (the double-axis
configuration), followed by a ring of CNOT gates that entangles nearest neighbours with
periodic boundary conditions. We refer to this entangler as \emph{hardware-efficient} in
the established sense of the term~\cite{kandala2017hardware}: it is built only from
one- and two-qubit gates that are native to current superconducting devices and it couples
only neighbouring qubits, so it could be executed on real hardware without the routing
overhead that an all-to-all entangler would incur. The term describes the gate layout
alone and carries no claim about the performance of the kernel. To limit excessive
expressivity that drives kernel concentration, the circuit relies on a shallow depth
(defaulting to a single repetition).

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{Figures/qsvm_circuit.pdf} % Assuming compiled PDF name
    \caption{Quantum feature map $U(x)$ utilized for the QSVM kernels, drawn for four
        qubits for legibility; the reported runs use $N_{\text{features}} = 12$ pooled
        features on 12 qubits. The scaled and bounded input features $\tilde{x}_q$ are
        encoded via $R_Y$ and $R_Z$ rotations on each qubit, followed by a
        nearest-neighbour ring of CNOT gates (a hardware-efficient entangler in the sense

\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Fidelity and Projected Quantum Kernels}
\label{sec:exp3_kernels}

The experiment evaluates two distinct quantum kernel formulations against the classical RBF baseline.

\textbf{Fidelity Kernel:} The standard overlap quantum kernel defines the similarity between two data points $x$ and $x'$ as the squared fidelity of their corresponding quantum states:
$$ k_F(x, x') = |\langle 0 | U^\dagger(x') U(x) | 0 \rangle|^2 $$
While geometrically intuitive, computing the full Gram matrix for $N$ samples requires $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ circuit executions, posing significant scaling challenges on hardware.

\textbf{Projected Quantum Kernel (PQK):} Following Huang et al. \cite{huang2021power}, we also evaluate the PQK, which projects the quantum state back into a classical representation by measuring local observables, reducing the circuit complexity to $\mathcal{O}(N)$. Specifically, the quantum state is measured in the Pauli $X$, $Y$, and $Z$ bases for each qubit, yielding a $3N_{\text{features}}$-dimensional vector of expectation values:
$$ p(x) = \bigcup_{q=1}^{N_{\text{features}}} \big\{ \langle X_q \rangle, \langle Y_q \rangle, \langle Z_q \rangle \big\} $$
A classical RBF kernel is then computed over these quantum-projected vectors. The RBF bandwidth ($\gamma$) for the PQK is resolved using the median heuristic on the pairwise squared distances of the projected training features. This approach is not explicitly prescribed in the original PQK formulation, but it is a common practice in classical kernel methods.

\subsubsection{Training Protocol and Pre-screening}
\label{sec:exp3_protocol}

The datasets are constructed from the targeted four-class subset of the SDSS DR19 dataset. The training set is subsampled to balanced sizes of $n \in \{2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200\}$ samples per class. The classical control is a Support Vector Classifier (SVC) equipped with an RBF kernel. To guarantee a fair comparison, the classical SVC undergoes a robust grid search over the regularization parameter $C \in \{0.1, 1, 10, 100\}$ and the bandwidth parameter $\gamma \in \{\text{scale}, 0.01, 0.1, 1.0\}$ at every evaluation step using stratified cross-validation. The projected kernel is an RBF kernel on quantum-projected features and therefore passes through this same tuned solver. The fidelity kernel instead supplies a precomputed Gram matrix and is fitted at a fixed $C = 1$; the quantity swept on its behalf is the encoding bandwidth $c$ of Section~\ref{sec:exp3_encoding}, evaluated after identifying $N_{\text{features}}$ over $c \in \{0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0, 1.5\}$.

Before initiating the compute-intensive scaling sweeps, we utilize a pre-training diagnostic metric: the geometric difference $g(K_C || K_Q)$ \cite{huang2021power}. Computed on the Gram matrices of a small data subset, this metric quantifies the divergence between the classical and quantum kernel geometries:
$$ g = \left\| \sqrt{K_Q} (K_C + \lambda I)^{-1} \sqrt{K_Q} \right\|_2 $$
where $\lambda$ is a regularization term evaluated across $\{10^{-6}, 10^{-8}, 10^{-10}\}$. If $g \approx 1$, the classical kernel seamlessly reproduces the quantum geometry, mathematically precluding a quantum advantage. We record $g$ prior to model fitting to empirically justify the search for advantage within the chosen encoding strategy.

\subsection{Experiment 4: Quanvolution and Noise Robustness}
\label{sec:exp4_methods}

\subsubsection{Design Principles}
\label{sec:exp4_philosophy}

The fourth experiment explores the hypothesised regularisation properties of quantum representation spaces under domain shift and adversarial noise. Classical convolutional neural networks, while highly expressive, are known to be vulnerable to high-frequency perturbations \cite{szegedy2013intriguing}. Variational quantum circuits, governed by unitary dynamics and bounded observable spectra, theoretically exhibit a restricted Lipschitz constant that may naturally resist such perturbations \cite{sokolic2017robust}.

To test this, we substitute standard classical convolutional filters with a parameter-matched Quanvolutional layer \cite{henderson2020quanvolutional}. The experiment enforces strict parameter parity to ensure any observed robustness arises intrinsically from the quantum geometry rather than a discrepancy in model capacity.

\subsubsection{Shared Architecture and Bounded Attention}
\label{sec:exp4_shared}

\begin{figure*}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Figures/quanvolution_model.png}
    \caption{Complete data flow and shared macro-architecture for Experiment 4. The 1D input flux is pooled, patched, and squashed via spherical encoding and trainable bounded attention. The patches are then processed by the contested middle stage (either the quantum or classical filter) before passing through a shared deep aggregation head containing 1D convolutions and an MLP to output binary classification logits.}
    \label{fig:exp4_macro}
\end{figure*}

Both the quantum and classical models share a unified macro-architecture, which is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:exp4_macro}. An input spectrum is first pooled to 1024 bins. Patches of size 4 are extracted with a stride of 2. To prevent the quantum wrap-around effect, where high-amplitude noise causes features to alias across $2\pi$ periodic boundaries, every patch $x$ is explicitly projected onto a hypersphere and scaled to a maximum phase of $\pi/2$:
$$ x' = \frac{x}{||x||_2 + \epsilon} \cdot \frac{\pi}{2} $$
Following this, the patches pass through a trainable Bounded Quantum Attention mechanism. A set of four learnable parameters is passed through a sigmoid function to create a bounding mask $m \in [0, 1]^4$, yielding the final encoded patch $\tilde{x} = x' \odot m$. This allows the network to selectively dampen individual spatial features within a patch without mathematically risking phase aliasing.

Regardless of the middle stage (quantum or classical), the filter maps the 4-dimensional patch to 12 output channels. These channels are subsequently processed by a shared deep aggregation head comprising two stages of 1D convolutions (expanding from 12 to 64 channels) interlaced with ReLU activations, followed by adaptive max pooling. A final Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) yields the binary classification logits.

\subsubsection{Quantum and Classical Filters}
\label{sec:exp4_filters}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{Figures/quanv_1D_layer.pdf}
    \caption{Variational quantum circuit utilized within the \texttt{Quanv1dLayer}. The bounded patch inputs $\tilde{x}_i$ are encoded via cyclically alternating Pauli rotations over three layers. A terminal uncoupled layer of general rotations acts as a learnable Quantum Lens prior to the extraction of the 12 channel expectations.}
    \label{fig:exp4_micro}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Quanvolutional Filter (\texttt{Quanv1dLayer}):} The quantum filter maps the 4-dimensional bounded patch $\tilde{x}$ into a 4-qubit variational circuit comprising $L=3$ layers. The internal gate structure of this filter is detailed in Figure~\ref{fig:exp4_micro}. To maximise feature distinctiveness across depths, the circuit employs a multi-axis data re-uploading strategy, where the encoding rotation shifts cyclically ($R_Y, R_Z, R_X$) per layer. Entanglement is generated via a robust ring of \texttt{IsingZZ} interactions, followed by general single-qubit \texttt{Rot} gates and a ring of \texttt{CNOT} gates.

Crucially, the circuit concludes with a "Quantum Lens"---a final uncoupled layer of trainable \texttt{Rot} gates. This lens is designed to allow the model to globally rotate the prepared quantum state into a measurement basis that is maximally orthogonal to the injected noise directions. The state is then measured across local Pauli $Z$, Pauli $X$, and two-body correlation $Z \otimes Z$ observables, yielding the 12 output channels. In total, the quantum filter contains 52 trainable parameters (36 rotation weights, 12 lens weights, and 4 attention weights).

\textbf{Parameter-Matched Classical Filter:} To serve as an honest baseline, the classical model replaces the quantum circuit with a lightweight Multi-Layer Perceptron. The network projects the 4-dimensional input through a hidden bottleneck of size 2, expanding out to 12 channels. Both layers utilise \texttt{Tanh} activations to strictly bound the classical outputs to $[-1, 1]$, mimicking the spectral bounds of the quantum Pauli expectations. This classical filter contains 46 trainable parameters, establishing an approximate parameter parity with the quantum filter.

\subsubsection{Generalisation and Noise Injection Protocols}
\label{sec:exp4_noise}

To assess robustness, the models are evaluated under two distinct training regimes over 5000 balanced samples:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Generalisation:} Models are trained exclusively on clean data. During evaluation, continuous Gaussian noise is incrementally injected into the test set spectra, scaled by the spectrum's standard deviation and a magnitude parameter $\sigma \in [0.0, 4.0]$. This tests the models' zero-shot resilience to atmospheric or instrumental degradation unseen during training.
    \item \textbf{Adversarial Training:} Models are exposed to dynamic noise during the training phase itself. A custom PyTorch dataset wrapper intercepts data batches, passing 50\% cleanly and injecting uniform random noise $\sigma \sim \mathcal{U}(0.1, 0.6)$ into the remaining 50\%. Crucially, the flux vectors are re-standard-scaled post-injection to preserve the angular boundaries of the spherical encoding. This regime tests whether the quantum filter's structural constraints allow it to learn a more robust underlying physical representation when explicitly subjected to perturbations.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Jacobian Smoothness Evaluation}
\label{sec:exp4_jacobian}

Beyond empirical accuracy under noise, we algorithmically quantify the Lipschitz smoothness of both filters. A highly sensitive feature map amplifies small input noises, whereas a smooth feature map dampens them. We extract a random subset of 300 test-set patches and compute the Jacobian matrix $J$ of the filter function $f: \mathbb{R}^4 \to \mathbb{R}^{12}$ with respect to the input patch $x$:
$$ J_{ij}(x) = \frac{\partial f_i(x)}{\partial x_j} $$
We report the median Frobenius norm $||J||_F$ across the sampled patches. A significantly lower Jacobian norm in the \texttt{Quanv1dLayer} serves as mathematical verification of the regularisation hypothesis, proving that the quantum map is intrinsically smoother with respect to its inputs than the classical counterpart.

\subsection{Experiment 5: True Quantum Convolutional Networks}
\label{sec:exp5_methods}

\subsubsection{Design Principles and the Quantum Data Caveat}
\label{sec:exp5_philosophy}

The final experiment shifts from hybrid classical-quantum models (such as the local sliding filters in \hyperref[sec:exp4_methods]{Experiment 4}) to true, fully coherent Quantum Convolutional Neural Networks (QCNNs) \cite{cong2019qcnn}. A true QCNN embeds the entire input vector into a single global quantum state and applies a hierarchy of weight-shared, translationally invariant unitaries, concluding with coherent pooling operations that progressively reduce the subsystem size. Theoretically, this architecture is proven to be immune to barren plateaus (vanishing gradients with depth) \cite{pesah2021absence} and exhibits strong inductive biases.

However, QCNNs were fundamentally designed for \textit{quantum data} (e.g., recognizing phases of quantum matter). Applying them to classical astronomical spectra requires embedding classical data into a quantum state. This experiment investigates whether the architectural robustness of the QCNN persists when the input is classically embedded flux. To ensure the integrity of the data representation, we restricted this experiment to a high signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) four-class subset, explicitly excluding Quasar (QSO) classes that lacked sufficient pristine samples, as excessive baseline noise fatally disrupts state preparation.

\subsubsection{Architectures: Pure QCNN vs. Classical Baselines}
\label{sec:exp5_arch}

\begin{figure*}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Figures/qcnn_model.png}
    \caption{Macro-architecture of the QCNN encoding ablation study. The input flux spectrum is routed through one of two distinct state preparation pipelines: Model A (Pure QCNN) applies $L_2$ normalization and amplitude-encodes the raw 4096-bin signal directly into 12 qubits, whereas Model B (Hybrid QCNN) utilizes a lightweight classical convolutional stem to compress the signal into 10 features before embedding them via multi-axis angle encoding. Both prepared states are then processed by an identical, weight-shared quantum convolutional and coherent pooling hierarchy. The three surviving qubits are measured across multiple Pauli bases and correlators, feeding a shared classical linear head to produce the final four class logits.}
    \label{fig:exp5_ablation_macro}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Amplitude-Encoded QCNN:} The primary model ingests the 4096-bin flux spectrum, applies $L_2$ normalization, and loads it into a 12-qubit register via Amplitude Encoding. The quantum feature map relies on two core variational blocks, illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:qcnn_blocks}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Convolution Block:} A parameterized $SU(4)$ unitary \cite{vidal2004su4} applied to pairs of adjacent qubits, requiring 15 trainable parameters. To enforce translational invariance and limit the parameter footprint, weights are heavily shared across the spatial dimension in a brickwork pattern with periodic boundary conditions.
    \item \textit{Pooling Block:} Coherent pooling is executed via controlled-$Z$ and controlled-$X$ rotations acting between a "source" and "sink" qubit pair. The source qubit is subsequently traced out (ignored), halving the active register size.
\end{itemize}
The complete hierarchy consists of an alternating sequence: a 12-qubit convolution, a pooling reduction to 6 qubits, a 6-qubit convolution, a pooling reduction to 3 surviving qubits, and a final deep convolution. The 3 surviving qubits are measured in the Pauli $X$, $Y$, and $Z$ bases, alongside $Z \otimes Z$ pairwise correlators, producing a rich classical feature vector. A linear layer maps these to the final 4 class logits. This extremely compact architecture totals roughly 101 trainable parameters.

\textbf{Classical Baselines:} Two classical controls were evaluated. The \textit{Classical Tiny Baseline} is a severely constrained 1D CNN with matched capacity (103 parameters), acting as the strict parity control. The \textit{Classical Huge Baseline} is an unconstrained, highly parameterized deep CNN, establishing the upper bound for classical performance on the same dataset. (8228 parameters)

(b) The coherent pooling block transfers amplitude from the source qubit to the sink qubit via controlled rotations, after which the source qubit is traced out, reducing the dimensionality of the quantum state.}

\subsubsection{The Encoding Ablation (Hybrid QCNN)}
\label{sec:exp5_ablation}

Amplitude encoding is notoriously susceptible to noise, as high-frequency perturbations alter the global $L_2$ norm, drastically reshaping the encoded quantum state. To determine whether empirical fragility arises from the QCNN architecture itself or the chosen embedding strategy, we designed an ablation model: the \textit{Hybrid QCNN}. In \ref{fig:exp5_ablation_macro} the same convolution and pooling blocks are retained, but the state preparation is modified.

This variant prepends a lightweight classical convolutional stem to compress the 4096-bin spectrum into 10 continuous features. These features are then embedded into a 10-qubit circuit using Angle Encoding (repeated three times via data re-uploading \cite{perezsalinas2020reuploading}). The quantum state is then subjected to the \textit{exact same} weight-shared quantum convolution and pooling logic as the pure QCNN. By comparing the robustness decay of the Pure QCNN against the Hybrid QCNN, we isolate the specific mathematical vulnerability of amplitude encoding.

\subsubsection{Evaluation Quadrants and Lipschitz Probing}
\label{sec:exp5_evaluation}

Models were evaluated across a matrix of four training quadrants: High-Data vs. Few-Shot (20 samples per class), and Clean Training vs. Adversarial Training (where dynamic noise $\sigma \sim \mathcal{U}(0.2, 0.6)$ was injected during gradient descent).

During inference, performance was measured via robustness decay: test accuracy was recorded as continuous Gaussian noise ($\sigma$) incrementally increased from $0.0$ to $4.0$. The overarching robustness metric is defined as the normalized Area Under the Curve (AUC) of the accuracy-versus-noise plot up to $\sigma=1.5$. Furthermore, we evaluated paired statistical significance using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

Finally, we algorithmically probed the local Lipschitz continuity of the models using Jacobian spectral norm analysis. For 128 randomly sampled test spectra, we computed the Jacobian matrix $J$ mapping the input space to the logit space. We report the median spectral norm $||J||_2$; a mathematically smaller spectral norm indicates a smoother manifold, providing a rigorous diagnostic for the network's intrinsic sensitivity to domain shifts.
